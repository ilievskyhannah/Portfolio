Where I Joined

An advocacy platform built in an emergency - fast sprints, no research. A post every 50 seconds across 15,000+ volunteers, 1.6M+ posts, 1.2B+ views. Platform-wide lifetime figures, not outcomes of the work below.

Two surveys - 579 respondents (Dec 2023) and 384 respondents (Mar 13 – Apr 29, 2024) - found the users weren't 16–30 as assumed. They were 50+. The team was already mid-redesign for that audience when I joined, right as the first survey's results came in - their direction was mobile; reading that same data is what led me to argue mobile-only wasn't enough, and to push for desktop. The second survey's finer brackets later confirmed it: the biggest single group was 61+ at 33.2%, and in a follow-up interview I found one user was 80 years old. The design team split by platform - the UX designer owned mobile, I owned desktop and all remaining resolutions - with one exception: the Personal Profile was my own initiative, and I built it end to end, across every breakpoint - mobile, tablet, desktop.

The Problems I Found

The product existed on one screen size.

Mobile-only, and broken on a wide screen. Nobody had raised it. I proposed the responsive expansion and argued for it internally - the one problem here I found before any research.

The other four came out of interviews I had to push for. The team trusted surveys and heatmaps. Initiating the study and selecting the 7 participants - by age, usage and tenure - were mine; the UX designer and I wrote the guide together, sat the sessions together, and worked through the findings as a pair. The guide was deliberately open: what do you like, what don't you.

  • Users were declining content silently. Not the feature - the content. It didn't always sit closely enough with their politics and values to carry their name.
  • No proof of impact. Volunteers shared with no evidence any of it landed.
  • No way to report a fault. Problems moved by hand - raised in an interview, messaged to me after, or spotted by an admin in the organization's WhatsApp group and sometimes never passed on.
  • The analytics were wrong. Failed shares counted as successes in the database. It had run for months.

Source: internal User Interviews Research Findings deck (Effectiveness, Bugs, and System Use sections).

How We Found the Solutions

“I look critically at the posts you send, and a lot of things I simply don't share. A lot of things.”

- PARTICIPANT #3, UNPROMPTED

Premade Comments had launched weeks earlier, so I asked about it. Usage turned out to be the smaller question - some used it, some didn't. Alignment was the real one, and it set the requirement: content the user chooses, by subject and by group.

To personalise content you have to know the user.
To know the user you need an account.
One finding built the whole account layer.

Registration and the Personal Profile weren't roadmap items. They were the price of personalization - and with a 50+ audience, that price had to be low enough to pay.

The Product

DESIGNED AND SHIPPED - TAP ANY SCREEN TO ENLARGE

What follows shipped and got used - 11,450+ sessions posting to Facebook and X. Full numbers further down.

Sharing Preferences

The silent rejection becomes an explicit, editable choice - subjects on one tab, groups on the other, each one reportable or removable straight from the list.

Registration

Split the ask. Three fields at the door, or one tap with Google - then birth year and country get filled in later, from the profile's edit screen, once there's a reason to ask.

Personal Profile

The volunteer's own replies and weekly shares, paired with the platform's collective total - personal impact alongside the bigger picture.

Reporting

Named reasons instead of a free-text box, so reports arrive already sorted - technical errors on one side, group-level problems like bans and denied posts on the other. No relay, no one to remember.

SHIPPED ALONGSIDE THE CORE FLOW

Update Notifications

For a 50+ audience an unannounced change reads as broken. Changes now arrive as information, before the encounter.

Seven Resolutions, Two Directions

The existing design system extended into a range it was never built for - button sizing, type scale, interaction states - plus a desktop navigation menu with no precedent in the product, in Hebrew RTL and English LTR throughout.

THE SWITCHER, ABSORBING A THIRD NETWORK POST-LAUNCH

What Held, and What Didn't

The Personal Profile went into production months after I left for Tel Aviv University - shipped with zero changes to my original design files. The team later added a third network to the Switcher; the pattern absorbed it without a redesign, which is a better test of a structure than how it looked at launch.

Desktop stayed under 5% of sessions (Clarity, May 2025). The interviews explained it: the way in was a WhatsApp group link, and a link in WhatsApp opens on a phone. Most users never knew a wide-screen version existed.

The constraint was distribution, not interface.

What I'd Do Differently

I expanded before I checked how people arrive.

The gap was real; the fix was wrong. Clarity would have shown a mobile-only base coming through one external link - the right first move was to bridge the path, not widen the destination. I don't regret making the argument. I regret not validating the assumption under it.

Next Steps

I left before the account layer had run long enough to measure. These are the numbers I would have watched, and each one maps to a decision on this page rather than to general engagement.

  • Preference-set rate at registration. The share of new accounts that choose at least one subject or group. Personalization was the reason the account layer exists - if people skip it, the account is cost without return.
  • Share-failure rate, now visible. Failed shares were counted as successes for months. With reporting in the product, the first honest number matters more than whether it looks good.
  • Reports per active volunteer. Whether faults now arrive through the product rather than through a relay - the test of whether the channel replaced the WhatsApp group or just sat beside it.
  • Desktop hand-off completion. The experiment I should have run first: offer a "continue on desktop" prompt with session hand-off, and measure how many take it. That answers whether the desktop share was a distribution problem or a demand problem.

Data tells you what happens.
Only people tell you why.

The finding that redirected this product came to a question I never asked. The same seven conversations found a metric reporting success for shares that never happened. Quantitative data can be confidently wrong - only people tell you which part.

In an advocacy platform,
the content is the interface.

When users judge each post as a decision about their own name, no amount of interface refinement reaches them - the thing being rejected isn't the screen. That reframing turned a uniform feed into content each volunteer authors, and it is the part of this project I would carry into any product where people put themselves behind what they publish.

Postscript, August 2026

Desktop and tablet use has grown substantially since I left - Clarity now shows 1,327 unique desktop users and 237 on tablet. By memory, not by export, the number during my time on the project was well under a hundred people, maybe as few as twenty. I don't know why it changed. No interviews ran after I left, and I can't rule out the whole platform simply growing alongside it. The number is real. The explanation is still the part only people could tell you.
