✦ Social & Profile • Case Study

Facts Commando

Platform that fights for truth — grounded in 2 surveys and 7 deep-dive qualitative interviews.

ROLE UX/UI Designer & Product Designer
TIMELINE Dec 2023 – Jul 2025

“In an advocacy platform,
the content is the interface.”

Joining a Live Product Mid-Course Correction

Facts Commando is a volunteer advocacy platform operating at scale — a post published every 50 seconds, across a network of 15,000+ volunteers, with 1.6M+ posts and 1.2B+ views to date. These are platform-wide figures spanning the product's full lifetime, not outcomes of the work described here.

POSTS 1.6M+
VIEWS 1.2B+
DIGITAL AMBASSADORS 15K+

The product was born in an emergency context and built rapidly in fast sprints without preliminary user research. As the user base expanded, the product grew organically — but without clear data on who was actually using it.

Two rounds of surveys — 579 respondents in the first, 384 in the second — revealed a crucial reality: users were not ages 16–30 as originally assumed, but 50+. The team was midway through a redesign to adapt the product for this demographic: larger typography, larger touch targets, explanatory onboarding, less visual noise, and direct functionality.

I joined mid-redesign. That direction was already set by the team; it wasn't mine. The design team split by platform — the UX designer on the team owned mobile, I owned desktop and all remaining resolutions. Two features we specified together, discussing structure and behaviour as a pair before each of us designed it for our own platform: the Premade Comments and the Social Media Switcher.

What I owned:

  • Responsive expansion — my initiative: proposed and drove the move from mobile-only to 7 screen resolutions. Extended the existing design system to desktop and large screens — desktop navigation menu, button sizing, type scale and interaction states — with RTL/LTR throughout.
  • Flows I owned end-to-end: registration and onboarding on mobile and desktop; the Personal Profile — impact tracking, profile editing, content preferences, technical issue reporting, and platform change notifications — specified from research through to production hand-off.
  • Specified with the UX designer, designed by platform: Premade Comments, and the Social Media Switcher when X was added alongside Facebook to widen the platform's reach.
  • Research: initiated and led the qualitative interview study, against initial team scepticism.

Making the Case for Responsive

The original user journey was linear: WhatsApp link → mobile web interface → one-tap copy → switch to Facebook. The interface wasn't a destination — it was a temporary station on the way.

The product was mobile-only, and there was no version of it for anyone on a wide screen; opening the link on a desktop computer produced a broken experience. Nobody had raised it. I proposed the responsive expansion, argued for it internally, and owned it end-to-end.

The design system already existed for mobile. My work was extending it into a resolution range it had never been built for — deciding how button sizing, type scale and interaction states should behave when the canvas triples, and designing the desktop navigation menu, which had no precedent in the product. Across seven distinct resolutions, every decision had to hold without breaking the mobile patterns the rest of the team was still building against.

The product also existed in both Hebrew (RTL) and English (LTR), requiring every component, header, and action button to reverse direction while remaining completely intuitive for a 50+ audience.

What happened after launch: desktop accounted for 2% of sessions against 98% mobile (Clarity, May 2025). The interface worked. It just wasn't where anyone arrived.

The interviews that followed explained why. The route into the product ran through a WhatsApp group link, and a link in WhatsApp opens on a phone. Users weren't choosing mobile over desktop — most didn't know a wide-screen version existed, and the habit was already formed.

The constraint was distribution, not interface. I had designed for users arriving directly at the product, when in practice there was only one path to it — and that path determined the device.

Pushing for Qualitative User Interviews

The team relied heavily on quantitative surveys and Clarity heatmaps, believing quantitative data was sufficient. I pushed to introduce in-depth qualitative interviews against initial scepticism, and I ran the study myself.

I designed the study end-to-end. I recruited from the survey respondents rather than from the team's contacts, and sampled against three strict criteria — age group, product usage frequency, and tenure with Facts Commando — so the sample covered both the users present since the emergency launch and those who arrived once the product had settled. Screening on tenure mattered: a volunteer from week one and a volunteer from month nine had joined two different products.

I wrote the guide, ran all 7 sessions myself, and synthesised the transcripts into the findings below. Running them personally is what made the difference — the most important sentence in the study came out of a follow-up question, not the script, and I would not have known to ask it from a summary. The findings uncovered product flaws that heatmaps could never have detected, and one of them turned out to be a bug in the analytics the team was relying on.

What Analytics Couldn't Catch

“I look critically at the posts you send, and a lot of things I simply don't share. A lot of things.”

— Deep interview, long-tenure user

That sentence reframed the product. Every quantitative measure we had treated the feed as a delivery mechanism and the user as a distributor. It wasn't. Users were reading each post as an editorial decision about their own name — would I put this in front of the people who know me? — and silently declining most of them.

In an advocacy platform, the content is the interface. Users act as editorial filters: if incoming content doesn't align with their personal values, they don't share it, and eventually they stop visiting. No amount of interface refinement addresses that, because the thing being rejected isn't the screen.

This is the finding that changed the product direction. It moved us from a uniform content feed — one stream, same for everyone, optimised for volume — to user-selected content customization, where a volunteer defines the causes and tones they're willing to put their name behind. That is a strategic change to what the product is, and it came out of seven conversations rather than any dashboard.

Three further findings came out of the same study:

  • WhatsApp is the entry engine. Notifications drive engagement; the product relies on external triggers rather than competing for constant attention. This is also what explains the 2% desktop figure — the entry point dictated the device, and no amount of responsive work could change that.
  • Users wanted proof their advocacy mattered. Volunteers were acting without any feedback loop — no sense of whether their sharing reached anyone. This finding directly spawned the Personal Profile and its impact tracking.
  • The data discrepancy bug. Interviews revealed that failed shares were being counted as successful shares in the database. The analytics the team trusted were reporting a reality that hadn't happened — heatmaps showed the clicks, but could not show that the metric behind them was broken.

What I Designed

Five flows came out of the research, and I owned all of them from specification through to hand-off. Each one exists because of a specific finding — none of them were on the roadmap before the interviews.

Registration and onboarding

The product had no account layer, and adding one to a live product with a 50+ audience is a point of maximum drop-off. I designed the full sign-up and onboarding flow for both mobile and desktop: minimal steps, high-contrast text, clear touch targets, and an onboarding that explained what an account gives you before asking for anything. Every field had to justify itself against the risk of losing a volunteer at the door.

The Personal Profile and impact tracking

Volunteers were acting with no feedback loop — no way to know whether their sharing reached anyone. The profile answers that directly: live exposure metrics and activity history, so a volunteer can see the effect of their own advocacy rather than the platform's aggregate. I also designed profile editing, so the account stays the user's to correct and maintain rather than a record they submit once and can't touch.

Content preferences

This is the interface answer to the study's central finding. Users were silently declining most of the feed because the content didn't match what they were willing to put their name behind. I designed the preference selection that lets a volunteer choose which content they receive, converting an invisible rejection into an explicit, editable choice — and turning a uniform feed into one the user has authored.

Technical issue reporting

The product had no reporting channel. Problems travelled through the WhatsApp group instead: a user mentioned something in the chat, a group admin noticed it, and the admin passed it up to the stakeholder. Every fault had to survive a chain of people choosing to relay it — and anything raised in a quiet moment, or by a user who didn't want to post in front of the whole group, simply never arrived.

I saw the size of the gap by accident. During the interviews, and for weeks afterwards, users began contacting me personally with technical problems — not because I had asked them to, but because I was the first person who had ever asked them anything. The reports weren't scarce. They had nowhere to go.

That is what convinced me this was a product problem rather than a support one. A reporting path that depends on someone remembering to pass a message will always under-report, and it under-reports worst for exactly the faults that matter — silent ones. The failed shares being counted as successes had been happening for months and reached the team through a research interview.

So I designed issue reporting directly into the profile, where users already were: a short, low-effort path from encountering a problem to reporting it, with no group audience and no intermediary. It replaced a human relay with a channel the product owned — which meant faults could reach the team from the people experiencing them, at the moment they experienced them.

Platform change notifications

A live product in an emergency context changes constantly, and users were discovering changes by encountering them. I designed notifications of platform changes so updates arrive as information rather than as surprise — which matters disproportionately for a 50+ audience, where an unannounced change reads as something broken rather than something new.

Designing Around Platform Dependency

When a product relies on third-party platforms, account blocks are an existential threat to active users — a volunteer who loses their Facebook account loses the entire reason they came. Designing in-app block reporting turned what looked like a support ticket into risk management infrastructure, embedded directly in the profile rather than hidden in a help page.

The same dependency shaped the Social Media Switcher. When X was added alongside Facebook, the switcher had to make the network an explicit user choice rather than a hidden default — and it had to be built as a pattern that could take additional networks, not a toggle between two. The UX designer and I specified the behaviour together and each designed it for our own platform.

What Shipped, and What Held

Production hand-off: the Personal Profile system I specified and designed went into production development months after I transitioned to Tel Aviv University — shipped with zero changes to my original design files.

The responsive expansion, the registration flow, and the Social Media Switcher are all live in the product today. The team later added a third network to the switcher, after I'd left. The pattern absorbed it without being redesigned — which is a more useful test of a structure than whether it looked right at launch.

What I'd Do Differently

The responsive expansion was my idea, and I expanded before I checked how people arrive.

I saw a gap — a product with no version for anyone on a wide screen — and I was right that it was a gap. I was wrong about the fix. Desktop reached 2% of sessions. The device split and entry path were knowable before I started: a look at Clarity would have shown a mobile-only user base arriving through a single external link. The right first move would have been to bridge the path rather than widen the destination — a “continue on desktop” prompt with session hand-off, tested before committing to a full responsive system. I don't regret making the argument. I regret not validating the assumption underneath it.

I inherited a persona and never rebuilt it.

Ezra predated me — built from the first survey, before I joined. What I did have was the material to replace him: a second survey of 384 respondents, and seven interviews where I knew each person's reasoning. I never went back and rebuilt the persona from that. A persona built from aggregation describes an audience; one built from interviews describes a person. The evidence for the better version was sitting in my own research files.

Key Takeaway

Data tells you what happens.
Only people tell you why.

I expanded the product before I checked how people reached it — and the thing that finally explained the numbers was seven conversations, not a dashboard. The same seven conversations found a metric that had been reporting success for shares that never happened. Quantitative data can be confidently wrong; only people tell you which part.
